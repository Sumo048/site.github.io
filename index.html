My bets

Research is a set of bets. Here are mine, roughly in order of how much I'd wager.

01

Deploy early.

Put learned policies in the real settings where they'll be used. That shows you the exact failures the policy will face, and it hands you high-quality on-policy data: both the failures and the good behaviour.

02

On-policy data wins.

After a year of continual-learning and forgetting experiments, my intuition is that what a fine-tuned policy becomes depends mostly on the fine-tuning data. Data that behaves like the base policy trains much better.

03

Scale data, even messy data.

Cheap, imperfect data is worth scaling, as long as you can make it look like something the policy would have done itself.

04

Reweight the prior, don't replace it.

Post-training should sharpen what pretraining gave you, not overwrite it.

05

Practical problem → principled question.

My favourite part of research is scoping. I like taking a messy real-world problem, finding the clean question inside it, and iterating fast. "Use planners in sim" is the practical idea; "make planner data on-policy for the base policy" is the question I can actually test.

What I'm working on now

Thread 1 · sim midtraining

Teacher data that looks like the student

A planner in simulation can solve tasks with no human in the loop, but its data looks nothing like what a pretrained policy would do, so fine-tuning on it fights the prior. Instead, I steer the frozen policy's own sampler toward the planner's intent. The data succeeds like the planner and looks like the policy. A planner gives the constraint; CS-Pi does the steering.

Questions I want to answer

  • Given a simulator and a base policy, what is the right data for midtraining?
  • What distribution of scenes should that data come from?
  • Can midtraining set up downstream RL, by optimising pass@k instead of pass@1?

Thread 2 · learning from deployment

Unlearning failures nobody saw coming

A big policy trained on lots of data will fail in ways nobody can predict. Only deployment exposes them. Successes are plentiful but mostly just reweight the policy; failures are rare and precious. My bet is that to remove a bad behaviour, you only need good behaviour at exactly the states where it failed, and the policy can often supply that itself. Think state-wise preferences, not trajectory-wise ones.

Also on my mind: touch and force inside the sim-data story, and robots that improve themselves from deployment.
